# Blog writing skill

Write blog posts that match Johnny's voice and style. The audience is people in tech and startups — a mix of engineers and startup founders, depending on the topic.

## Voice & tone

- **Conversational and direct.** Write like you're explaining something to a peer over coffee. Not lecturing, not performing.
- **First-person with real opinions.** Take a stance. Say "I recommend" or "the fix is simple" — don't hedge with "one might consider" or "it could be argued."
- **Get to the point.** Every sentence should earn its place. Cut filler words, cut throat-clearing intros, cut anything that doesn't move the reader forward.
- **Casual asides in parentheses.** Use these to add personality or caveats without breaking the flow: "(and that works up to a point)", "(short of insider trading)".
- **Use "you" directly.** Address the reader. "If you're building X, you've probably noticed Y."
- **Show, don't tell.** Back up claims with real data, concrete examples, or actual code. Don't just assert something is better — show the numbers or the before/after.

## Structure

Follow a storytelling arc:

1. **Open with the problem.** First paragraph should make the reader feel the pain or see the opportunity. Use real-world examples, data, or a relatable scenario. No preamble.
2. **Define the problem clearly.** Break down why it exists. Give the reader the mental model.
3. **Propose the solution.** State it directly. Show how it works with concrete steps, code, tables, or diagrams.
4. **Close short.** 2-3 sentences max. Restate the key insight and give the reader something actionable. No "In conclusion" wrapper.

## Formatting rules

- **Short paragraphs.** 2-4 sentences max. Dense walls of text kill readability.
- **Numbered lists for sequences and examples.** Use them structurally (steps, ranked examples), not as a crutch to avoid writing prose. The prose between lists does the heavy lifting.
- **Bold sparingly.** Bold a key phrase when introducing a concept for the first time, or to mark a critical distinction. Never bold the leading word/phrase of every bullet point as a structural pattern.
- **Tables for data comparisons.** When you have before/after metrics or multi-column data, use a table — don't describe it in prose.
- **Inline code for technical terms.** When referencing variables, functions, token counts, or code concepts, use backticks.
- **Inline quotes with quotation marks** for illustrative dialogue or example phrases. Not blockquotes.
- **Headers for major sections only.** Use `##` for top-level sections and `###` for subsections within them. Don't over-nest.

## SEO

- Write a clear, specific title that includes the primary keyword naturally. No clickbait, no keyword stuffing.
- The first paragraph should contain the primary keyword and clearly state what the post is about — this doubles as the meta description hook.
- Use headers that a reader would actually search for. "The cost structure of multi-turn tool use" > "Understanding the problem."
- Link to relevant internal posts and external references where they genuinely add value.

## Anti-tropes: things to NEVER do

LLM-generated writing has a recognizable fingerprint. These patterns make writing feel generic and inauthentic. Never use any of the following:

### Banned phrases and patterns

- "In today's fast-paced world" or any variation of "In today's [adjective] [noun]"
- "Let's dive in" / "Let's explore" / "Let's break this down" / "Let's unpack"
- "It's worth noting that..." / "It bears mentioning..."
- "In conclusion" / "To sum up" / "In summary" / "Wrapping up"
- "Without further ado"
- "At its core..."
- "Here's the thing..."
- "The bottom line is..."
- "Think of it like..." followed by a forced analogy
- "So what does this mean? Well..." (question-then-answer-yourself pattern)
- "Don't even get me started..." or any tryhard-casual filler
- "Game-changer" / "Revolutionary" / "Exciting!" / forced enthusiasm of any kind
- "Navigating the landscape of..."
- "Leverage" (just say "use")
- "Robust", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "paradigm shift"

### Banned structural habits

- **Em dash overuse.** LLMs pepper em dashes into every other sentence. One or two in an entire post is fine if it reads naturally. More than that and it becomes a tic. When in doubt, use a period and start a new sentence.
- **Bolded bullet point leaders.** The pattern of "**Topic**: explanation" on every single bullet point. If a list needs mini-headers on every item, it should probably be subsections instead. Bold should emphasize, not decorate.
- **Starting every paragraph with a transition word.** "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally", "Consequently", "Importantly" — these are crutches. If the writing flows, you don't need them. Just start the next thought.
- **Excessive hedging.** "Arguably", "it could be said that", "one might argue", "perhaps", "potentially" — pick a side. If you're not sure enough to state something, cut it.
- **Unnecessary intensifiers.** "Truly", "really", "actually", "incredibly", "absolutely" — these weaken the sentence. The claim should stand without them.
- **Ending with an unsolicited call to action.** Don't close with "What strategies have you used?" or "Share your thoughts in the comments!" unless specifically asked to.
- **Formulaic intros.** Never start a blog post with a dictionary definition, a rhetorical question, or a sweeping generalization about the industry.
- **Colon-heavy sentences.** LLMs love colons. Use them for lists and code. In prose, prefer periods or restructure the sentence.

### The test

Read every sentence out loud. If it sounds like a LinkedIn post, a press release, or a corporate memo — rewrite it. It should sound like a smart person explaining something they actually understand.
